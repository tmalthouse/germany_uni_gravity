\section{Student flows}

Following \textcite{HEAD2014131}, the main specification follows the general gravity form
\begin{equation}
F_{odt} = G \, S_o \, M_d \, \phi_{od} \, \eta_{odt},
  \qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\eta_{odt} \mid \cdot\right] = 1,
\label{eq:gravity_general}
\end{equation}
with bilateral term
\begin{equation}
\phi_{od} = \mathrm{dist}_{od}^{\,\beta_1}
  \exp\!\Big(
      \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od}
      + \beta_3 \mathrm{SameState}_{od}
      + \beta_4 \mathrm{SamePolity}_{od}
    \Big).
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
Origin and destination fixed effects absorb $\ln G + \ln S_o$ and $\ln M_d$, giving estimating equation
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od}
   + \beta_3 \mathrm{SameState}_{od}
   + \beta_4 \mathrm{SamePolity}_{od}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:gravity_baseline}
\end{equation}

``SameState'' indicates whether the origin-destination pair lies within the same sovereign state. ``SamePolity'' indicates whether the origin-destination pair lie within the same province, district, or similar subnational division---this name was chosen because only Prussia had provinces \emph{per se}, but other states had analagous administrative divisions. ``SameCity'' indicates that a student's hometown matches the city of their university.

In analysis (2) the border effect is split by confessional pairing of origin and university, with the baseline being different-state, different-confession OD pairs, giving estimating equation
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od}
   + \beta_4 \mathrm{SamePolity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \lambda_{\mathrm{SS,SC}} \mathrm{SS\text{-}SC}_{od}
   + \lambda_{\mathrm{SS,DC}} \mathrm{SS\text{-}DC}_{od}
   + \lambda_{\mathrm{DS,SC}} \mathrm{DS\text{-}SC}_{od}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:gravity_confession}
\end{equation}
Both specifications are estimated using Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML), as is standard in this literature, since many origin-destination-year flows are zero.

Note that confession is derived from location, as micro-level confessional data are only available for Heidelberg students. For example, the Prussian Rhineland is a majority-Catholic region in a majority-Protestant state, so students coming from that region attending Berlin would be classified as ``same state, different confession;'' a student from the region attending Freiburg would be classified as ``different state, same confession.'' This is not a perfect metric---many regions had both confessions well-represented, and we know nothing about the behaviour of Jewish students. The appendix presents a robustness exercise looking only at Heidelberg students whose individual religion is known (TODO).

The recovered distance elasticity is similar to the value found in papers studying the modern student market; the very large ``same state'' coefficient indicates that political boundaries were very important in shaping the student market. \\
   Specification (2) decomposes the ``same state'' effect, examining whether it is driven by the state border itself or religious homophily. It is possible that the student market changed over the sample period. Improvements in transportation technology may cause the distance elasticity to decrease; political changes or a changing student body may also have some effect. This section tests whether the two main parameters estimated in the previous section vary by decade.

All specifications detailed in this section build on specification (1) in table~\ref{tab:gravity_structure}. Specifications are estimated by PPML, with standard errors clustered by origin, $e$ indexes the decade, $t$ the year of first enrolment, $o$ the student's hometown, and $d$ their destination university. The distance and state border terms are interacted with decade dummies, with 1800--09 being used as the baseline. Destination-decade fixed effects absorb each university's enrolment level. The estimating equation used to create figure \ref{fig:era_coefficients} is then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{ode} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_{de}
   + (\beta_1 + \lambda_e) \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + (\beta_3 + \mu_e) \mathrm{SameState}_{od}
   + \beta_4 \mathrm{SamePolity}_{od},
   \qquad \lambda_1 = \mu_1 = 0,
\end{split}
\label{eq:gravity_by_decade}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \ffigbox
  {\caption{These figures examine whether the core parameters of the gravity model, the distance elasticity and same-state effect, vary over time. Estimated by equation \ref{eq:gravity_by_decade}, these plots show the interaction term of the parameter with decade dummies. As the first plot shows, there was a slight increase in the state border effect (not statistically different from flat) in the 1920s, slightly decreasing after that; the second plot shows that the distance elasticity decreases very slightly with time (but also not statistically differing from a constant elasticity.)}
   \label{fig:era_coefficients}}
  {\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{era_coefficients.png}}
\end{figure}

The elasticity of distance is nearly flat over the sample period, with a slight decrease over time. The same-state effect increases somewhat after 1820, although not enough to reject the hypothesis of a constant effect.

A possible cause of this increase are the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819. These policies were intended to decrease radical activity (including support for German unification) at universities, led to the dismissal of liberal faculty members, and banned many student organizations. This may have some effect on the border effect, since it would be politically ``safer.'' I test this by estimating
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_{dt}
   + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + (\beta_3 + \theta_t) \mathrm{SameState}_{od},
   \qquad \theta_{t_0} = 0,
\end{split}
\label{eq:event_study_1819}
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is the first year of the sample. Figure~\ref{fig:event_study_1819}
plots the path $\beta_3 + \theta_t$, the by-year state border effect.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \ffigbox
  {\caption{An event study examining whether the reactionary Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 could be linked to the increased same-state effect, as estimated by equation \ref{eq:event_study_1819}. This theory can be confidently rejected: the increase in propensity to attend a same-state university takes place in the preceding five years, and appears to level off after the decrees are passed.}
   \label{fig:event_study_1819}}
  {\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{event_study_1819.png}}
\end{figure}

The Carlsbad Decrees were not the cause of the increase in the state border effect. The increase began roughly five years earlier, with no further changes following the policy change.

\section{The new universities}

Two new universities are founded in the sample period. Berlin is established in 1810 and Bonn in 1818, both as intentionally ``modern'' universities with a more rigourous curriculum than existing universities. It may be that these universities draw students from further away and have a lower distance elasticity. I test this using specification
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{ode} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \delta_e
   + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \sum_{k \in \{\text{Berlin}, \text{Bonn}\}}
     \Big( \kappa_k \mathbf{1}[d = k] \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
     + \sum_{e} \pi_{ke} \mathbf{1}[d = k] \Big),
\end{split}
\label{eq:design_b_static}
\end{equation}
For each new university $k$, $\kappa_k > 0$ means that institution's distance elasticity is smaller in magnitude than the old universities'---that is, students travel from further away to attend.

It may also be the case that these relative distance gradients vary over time, as these universities build reputations or incumbent institutions are better able to compete. In this specification, for the new institutions, I consider only flows after the founding of the university for which I have data---that is, only the 1820--48 subsample. The estimating equation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{ode} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_{de}
   + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \sum_{k \in \{\text{Berlin}, \text{Bonn}\}} \; \sum_{e \geq 3}
     \kappa_{ke} \mathbf{1}[d = k] \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od},
\end{split}
\label{eq:design_b_dynamic}
\end{equation}
Note that destination-by-decade fixed effects hold the level by decade fixed, so increasing enrolment will not change $\kappa_{ke}$ if the underlying elasticity does not change as well. As before, $\kappa_{ke} > 0$ indicates that university $k$ in decade $e$ has a lower distance elasticity and broader reach than the sample as a whole.

\section{The Göttingen Seven}

My hypothesis is that the introduction of ``modern'' unversities changed the geography of student flow. In the previous section, we see that the newly-founded universities in Berlin and Bonn had a farther reach than incumbent universities, suggesting students were willing to travel further for their program. In this section, I perform an event study on the ``G\"ottingen Seven'' incident, where in 1837 the Kingdom of Hanover fired seven prominent liberal professors at the University of G\"ottingen. This was a prestigious institution; did this incident affect its ability to draw students?

I perform a single-destination event study with 1837 as the reference year---the firings and subsequent protests happened in November, after that year's cohort had enroled. The estimating equation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \delta_t
   + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \sum_{y \neq 1837} \tau_y \,
     \mathbf{1}[d = \text{Göttingen}] \, \mathbf{1}[t = y].
\end{split}
\label{eq:got7_event}
\end{equation}
The year fixed effects absorb any Germany-wide economic and cohort shocks, so $\tau_y$ gives G\"ottingen's enrolment relative to other universities in year $y$.

I also differentiate ``near'' and ``far'' students, setting 200km as the dividing line (TODO: robustness checks on this). Many nearby students likely attended because G\"ottingen was the most convenient option or because they had university-specific networks; if there is an effect, it is likely to come from the distanct students who specifically sought out the university. This specification is estimated by
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \delta_t
   + \beta_1 \ln \mathrm{dist}_{od}
   + \beta_2 \mathrm{SameCity}_{od} \\
  &\quad + \sum_{g \in \{\text{near}, \text{far}\}}
     \Big( \psi_g G_{odg}
     + \sum_{y \neq 1837} \tau_{gy} \, G_{odg} \, \mathbf{1}[t = y] \Big),
\end{split}
\label{eq:got7_heterogeneity}
\end{equation}
where $G_{odg} = \mathbf{1}[d = \text{Göttingen}] \, \mathbf{1}[o \in g]$. The $\tau_{gy}$ are the coefficients of interest: the difference in enrolment at G\"ottingen compared to other universities for near or far students.

I then ask where the lost students went. Let $\mathcal{O}$ be a set of origins and
$\pi$ a period, either $\mathrm{pre} = 1834$--37 or $\mathrm{post} = 1838$--41.
The share of that group's enrolments going to university $d$, and the change in
that share across the two periods, are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
s_d^{\pi}(\mathcal{O})
  &= \frac{\sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}} \sum_{t \in \pi} F_{odt}}
          {\sum_{d'} \sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}} \sum_{t \in \pi} F_{od't}}, \\
\Delta s_d(\mathcal{O})
  &= s_d^{\mathrm{post}}(\mathcal{O}) - s_d^{\mathrm{pre}}(\mathcal{O}).
\end{split}
\label{eq:got7_shares}
\end{equation}
These are accounting identities rather than estimates, and no university serves as a reference category, which is why every destination appears in
figure~\ref{fig:goettingen7_reallocation} and
table~\ref{tab:goettingen7_reallocation}. The treated set is the 100 far origins
($\geq 200$\,km from G\"ottingen) that sent at least one student there in
1834--37; the near origins, defined the same way, serve as a comparison group.

Table~\ref{tab:goettingen7_reallocation_ppml} tests whether Berlin and Bonn's enrolment significantly changed as a result of this incident.
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ln \mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{odt} \mid \cdot\right]
  &= \alpha_o + \gamma_d + \delta_{p(t)} \\
  &\quad + \rho_{\text{G}} \, \mathbf{1}[d = \text{Göttingen}] \, \mathrm{Post}_{p(t)}
   + \rho_{\text{N}} \, \mathbf{1}[d \in \{\text{Berlin}, \text{Bonn}\}] \, \mathrm{Post}_{p(t)},
\end{split}
\label{eq:got7_reallocation}
\end{equation}
where $p(t)$ is the period containing year $t$; the flows are annual, and only the
period terms and their fixed effect are coarsened. The remaining old
universities are the omitted category, but they are not dropped. They stay in the sample with their own destination fixed effects, and $\rho_{\text{G}}$ and
$\rho_{\text{N}}$ measure the relative post-period change.
